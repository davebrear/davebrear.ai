The above diagram explains how my second brain is structured. The method is based on the Zettelkasten, a system developed by the sociologist Niklas Luhmann and brilliantly explained in Sonke Ahrens' [How to Take Smart Notes](https://smartnotes.teachable.com/p/my-downloadable-166384), the book that changed how I think about note-taking. Ideas enter through the inbox: a quote, a podcast timestamp, a thought on a walk. They get grounded against source material: books, articles, conversations. Then they become atomic notes: single ideas, written in my own words, linked to each other. The links are where the thinking happens. Over time, clusters of connected atoms assemble into outputs: the articles on this site.

The whole network is public. There are two ways to use it.

## Connect your AI

Add `davebrear.ai/mcp` as a connector in Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, or any MCP-compatible tool. Your AI can then explore my thinking, follow the links between notes, and reason across the network.

Try asking your AI:

- *"Using davebrear.ai, what does Dave think about AI and critical thinking? Follow the links."*
- *"What's the relationship between the four modalities of search?"*
- *"Find the stub notes and tell me which open questions are most interesting."*

Your conversations stay private. I can't see them. The responses come through your own AI, not a canned system.

## Fork it and make it yours

The vault is a [public GitHub repo](https://github.com/davebrear/davebrear-ai). Fork it, clone it, point your AI at it, and start adding your own atomic notes. My thinking becomes the seed for yours.

There are deliberately unfinished notes with open questions. They're invitations to think:

- What happens when everyone has a second brain?
- Where does the human end and the AI begin?
- Can a second brain be shared?

Pick one. Disagree with it. Extend it. Answer the question I couldn't. That's how a Zettelkasten grows.

The [getting-started guide](https://github.com/davebrear/davebrear-ai/blob/main/GETTING-STARTED.md) walks through the full process in 10 minutes.

## What's inside

94 atomic notes, 10 maps of content, 11 published articles, 13 source references, and 1 podcast transcript. All cross-linked. The same method I write about is the method that built the vault. [Explore the knowledge graph](/graph/) to see how everything connects.

A **map of content** (MOC) is a bridging note. When I notice a cluster of atoms on a topic, I create a MOC that organises them into a chain of cognition: this idea leads to that one, which connects to a third. Every article in the vault has one. The MOC is the spine that turns scattered atoms into an argument - the writing becomes assembly, not invention.

This is an evolving vault. I'll update it with new atomic notes and articles as I create them. The network grows over time.

## Where my head is right now

**Compression dominates the conversation. Expansion is where the value sits.** Nearly all the enterprise AI talk is about compression: automate the workflow, do it faster, remove a human. That's useful for the work that can be compressed. But the workflows that need real thinking can't be, and that's where the value is - pointing AI at a growing context vault to expand and pressure-test my thinking, lifting the quality of the output rather than the volume. It was the spine of my half of the [first Citrix AI Hotsheet episode](/posts/citrix-ai-hotsheet-episode-1/) with Brian Madden.

**Subscribable brains as a new medium.** Publishing my vault as an MCP endpoint felt different from publishing an article. An article gets read once; a subscribable brain gets queried repeatedly, by AIs I'll never see. I'm writing for traversal now, not just for reading. It also answers a question I kept circling: can a second brain be shared? Merging two vaults flattens two perspectives into one. Subscribable brains keep them discrete - my vault stays mine, yours stays yours, and an AI traverses across both. I tested it by subscribing to [Brian Madden's MCP](https://brianmadden.ai) alongside my own. Two brains, one session, perspectives intact.

**The governance problem is closer than people think.** Every conversation with enterprise customers surfaces the same tension: individuals building powerful context layers their organisations can't see, govern, or retain. Shadow AI is already here. Two things sharpened it lately: the real exposure is aggregation (individually harmless thoughts synthesise into something genuinely company-sensitive), and Brian and I have now moved our own second brains into Citrix-governed systems, above board, rather than running them as personal skunkworks. Same lesson as shadow IT - you don't win by blocking, you win by providing a sanctioned path.

**Two halves that might fit together (still chewing on this).** On the Hotsheet, Brian's half was about agents that act inside the same apps and environments humans already use. My half was about context vaults and expansion. We didn't plan it, but the two halves kept reaching for each other: an agent that knows me through my context vault and can also act inside my actual governed environment is a different proposition to either piece alone. Not settled yet, but it gave me something to ponder.

**It's a practice gap, and it's quietly widening.** Models became genuinely good at this around November 2025, when writable memory turned a chatbot into a collaborator, and a small group of pioneers independently arrived at the same patterns. Even if capabilities froze today, there's a decade of work left to exploit what's already here. Most people still use AI as a search replacement; a handful have figured out persistent collaboration. The gap between them is a practice gap, not a technology gap - and practice gaps close through imitation, not invention. When colleagues start asking "how did you produce that?" the tipping point isn't far behind.

**The thesis has matured from a name into a body of work.** The umbrella is Augmented Cognition; the method within it is Chain of Cognition; the enemy it defeats is Reheated Thinking and the meaning-shaped slop it leaves behind. The clearest way I explain the difference: the same AI is a freezer or a sous-chef depending on the posture I take toward it. As a freezer of pre-cooked positions it reheats; as a sous-chef under my direction it helps me cook. The microwave didn't change, the cook did. I'm building it out a piece a week: [Cook, Don't Reheat](/posts/cook-dont-reheat/) named the enemy, [Chain of Cognition](/posts/chain-of-cognition-how-to-cook-instead/) named the method, and [How the Sausage Was Made](/posts/how-the-sausage-was-made/) showed it in action.
